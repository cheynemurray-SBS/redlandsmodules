Populations & Samples

Why statistical inference?

In business, we rarely measure everyone. A retailer cannot survey every potential customer; a manufacturer cannot test every unit. Statistical inference lets us draw reliable, quantified conclusions about a large population from a smaller, well-chosen sample — and attach a precise measure of our uncertainty.

Population

The entire group of interest — every customer, every transaction, every unit produced. Often too large or expensive to measure fully.

μ (mu) — meanσ — std devN — size

Sample

A manageable, randomly selected subset. Sample statistics estimate population parameters. Validity depends on how the sample was drawn.

x̄ — means — std devn — size

Two branches of inference

Estimation

Use sample statistics to estimate population parameters. Produces a point estimate or an interval estimate — a confidence interval with an attached probability.

Hypothesis Testing

Evaluate a specific claim about a population using sample evidence. Has our average order value changed? Does the new process outperform the old?

MBA decision context

Before committing resources, pricing changes, or operational shifts, executives need to know whether data differences reflect real patterns or mere sampling variation. Inference transforms sample evidence into defensible, probabilistic business decisions.

Sample Size

The precision dial

Larger samples reduce uncertainty but cost more time and money. The right sample size balances statistical precision against operational constraints. Getting this right before data collection is one of the highest-leverage analytical decisions a manager can make.

Required sample size — estimating a mean

n = ( z* · σ / E )²

Where z* is the critical value for your confidence level, σ is the estimated population standard deviation, and E is the maximum acceptable margin of error.

Sampling Distribution of x̄

The Central Limit Theorem (CLT)

If we repeatedly drew samples of size n from the same population and plotted each sample mean, those means form the sampling distribution of x̄. The CLT tells us this distribution is approximately normal for sufficiently large n — regardless of the population’s shape.

Business intuition

Even if individual customer purchases are highly skewed, the average across a random sample of n ≥ 30 will be approximately normally distributed. This is what makes inference valid even for non-normal business data.

Confidence Intervals

What is a confidence interval?

A confidence interval (CI) is a range of values constructed from sample data likely to contain the true population parameter. A 95% CI means: if we repeated this sampling process 100 times, approximately 95 of the resulting intervals would capture the true mean.

CI = x̄ ± z* · (s / √n)     [use t* when σ unknown or n < 30]

★ Common misinterpretation — avoid in any presentation

A 95% CI does not mean there is a 95% probability the true mean is in this specific interval. The true mean is fixed; the interval varies. Correct: “We are 95% confident this interval captures the true population mean.”

Hypothesis Testing

The logic of hypothesis testing

We start by assuming the null hypothesis H₀ is true (“nothing has changed”), then ask: how probable is our observed result under that assumption? If very unlikely, we have grounds to reject H₀ in favor of the alternative H₁.

The five-step framework

  1. State the hypotheses. H₀ asserts the status quo; H₁ is the alternative you aim to support with evidence.
  2. Choose significance level α. Typically 0.05 — the maximum probability of a Type I error you will accept.
  3. Calculate the test statistic. Converts sample data into a standardized score relative to what H₀ predicts.
  4. Find the p-value. Probability of observing a result as extreme — or more so — if H₀ were true.
  5. Decision. If p ≤ α → reject H₀. If p > α → fail to reject H₀ (insufficient evidence, not proof H₀ is true).

Statistical vs. practical significance

A result can be statistically significant but operationally irrelevant. With n = 10,000, even a $0.02 difference may reach p < 0.05. Always ask: is the effect size large enough to justify the response? Report effect size alongside p-values in executive presentations.

One-Sample t-test

When to use it

The one-sample t-test applies when: (1) you have a single sample, (2) population SD σ is unknown, and (3) you want to test whether the sample mean differs significantly from a hypothesized value μ₀. The t-distribution with n − 1 degrees of freedom accounts for the uncertainty of estimating σ.

t = (x̄ − μ₀) / (s / √n)     df = n − 1

Assumptions to verify

  1. Data are approximately normally distributed or n ≥ 30 (CLT allows relaxing normality)
  2. Observations are independent — random sampling with no pairing or clustering
  3. No extreme outliers that would substantially distort the sample mean

Connection: t-test ↔ confidence interval

Rejecting H₀ at α = 0.05 (two-tailed) is mathematically equivalent to the hypothesized value μ₀ falling outside the 95% CI. The CI and the t-test are two perspectives on the same evidence.
